Material Submittals

Verify the Substance Before Approving the Form

Material submittals are the first technical filter — verifying that what the contractor proposes to install matches the specification in substance, not just in name. Every submittal is reviewed against IFC drawings, technical datasheets, and physical samples. The output is binary: Approved, Approved with Comments, or Rejected. No intermediate status exists on a well-run site.

Key Actions
  • Verify IFC alignment — does the proposed material match the approved spec?
  • Review technical datasheets against performance requirements
  • Inspect physical samples under site-representative lighting conditions
  • Issue formal Approved / Approved with Comments / Rejected status

Shop Drawings

Where It Goes — Coordination & Alignment

Shop drawings translate the design intent into fabrication and installation reality. They show exact dimensions, fixing details, coordination with adjacent trades, and buildability logic. Key Actions

  • Check coordination with structural, MEP, and facade drawings

Method Statement

How It Will Be Done — Safe, Sequenced, Controlled

The Method Statement defines the complete execution strategy before a single worker mobilizes. It must specify the step-by-step construction sequence, protective safety measures, required equipment and manpower, and integration with the Inspection and Test Plan (ITP). If the method is wrong, the output will be wrong — regardless of material quality. The architect reviews for compliance with specifications, safety, and sequence logic.

Key Actions
  • Verify construction sequence complies strictly with specifications
  • Review safety plan: protective measures and risk mitigation confirmed
  • Confirm equipment and manpower allocations are adequate for scope
  • Map ITP inspection checkpoints against method statement milestones

Mockup / Sample Approval

Test Once Before Repeating 1,000 Times

The physical mockup is the last filter before mass execution. It translates approved materials and methods into a real, inspectable result at full scale. This is the point where misalignments, finish discrepancies, unseen coordination issues, and workmanship shortfalls are exposed — before they multiply across the entire project. Rejecting a mockup costs days. Rejecting mass-installed work costs months and millions.

Key Actions
  • Inspect alignment: levels, joint widths, and setting-out accuracy
  • Verify finish quality: no defects, scratches, or contamination
  • Assess colour and texture against approved sample under natural and artificial light
  • Evaluate overall workmanship standard — this becomes the benchmark for all future work

Site Execution

Key Actions

Section 02
Pre-Execution & Approval Documents

These documents are normally required before starting an activity on site. Their purpose is to prevent wrong execution before it happens.

MS
Method Statement
Execution method
Explains the approved step-by-step method for carrying out a specific work activity. It describes manpower, equipment, tools, materials, access, safety controls, sequence of work, protection of adjacent works and quality checks. Site teams use it as the official execution procedure, and QA/QC uses it to verify that the work was done in the approved way.
ITP
Inspection and Test Plan
Inspection & testing plan
Defines the inspection stages for an activity and the acceptance criteria for each stage. It identifies who checks the work, what documents are required, which tests must be performed, and where the consultant must stop the work for hold point approval. It is the bridge between execution and formal acceptance.
QCP
Quality Control Plan
Project quality plan
Describes the full project quality system, including roles, inspection workflow, documentation control, testing procedures and reporting lines. It does not focus on one activity only; it sets the overall QA/QC framework that all ITPs, IRs, NCRs and checklists must follow.
PQD
Pre-Qualification Documents
Supplier / subcontractor qualification
Demonstrates that a subcontractor, manufacturer or specialist supplier is capable of performing the required scope. It normally includes company profile, past experience, licenses, certificates, financial/technical capability, method capability and project references before appointment or approval.
MAR / MTS
Material Approval Request / Material Technical Submittal
Material approval package
Submitted before procurement or installation to prove that the proposed material complies with drawings, specifications and project requirements. It usually includes datasheets, catalogues, samples, certificates, compliance statements, test reports and warranty information.
MAS
Material Approval Sheet
Material approval record
A formal sheet or cover page showing the consultant response to a material submittal. It records whether the material is approved, approved with comments, revised and resubmitted, or rejected. It becomes a reference for procurement and material inspection on site.
SD
Shop Drawing
Detailed execution drawing
Prepared by the contractor or specialist subcontractor to convert design drawings into buildable installation drawings. It includes real dimensions, details, fixing methods, levels, setting-out, coordination with structure/MEP and site constraints. No major work should proceed without approved shop drawings.
Mock-up
Mock-up Approval
Approved sample installation
A physical sample area constructed on site to confirm workmanship, appearance, materials, joints, tolerances and coordination before mass installation. Once approved, it becomes the benchmark for acceptance of the same work across the project.
Sample Board
Material Sample Board
Board of approved samples
Displays physical samples such as tile, stone, paint, metal, wood, glass, fabric or sealant. It helps the client and consultant approve color, finish, texture and quality. Later, site deliveries are compared against the approved board to avoid substitutions.
HP
Hold Point
Mandatory stop point
A critical inspection stage where the contractor must stop work and cannot proceed until the consultant/client has inspected and approved. Example: waterproofing inspection before screed, rebar inspection before concrete, or substrate approval before finishing.
WP
Witness Point
Optional witnessing point
A planned stage where the consultant is invited to witness an inspection or test. Depending on the contract, if the consultant does not attend within the agreed notice period, the contractor may proceed while keeping records and evidence.
Checklist
Inspection Checklist
Detailed checklist
A practical list of items to verify during inspection. It translates drawings, specifications, ITP criteria and method statement requirements into measurable site checks such as alignment, level, thickness, spacing, fixing, cleanliness and protection.

These documents control inspection, acceptance, rejection, corrective action and quality records during site execution.

IR
Inspection Request
Request to inspect work
A formal request submitted by the contractor to the consultant QA/QC team to inspect completed work. It creates a record that the work was offered for approval before covering it, closing it, or proceeding to the next construction stage.
WIR
Work Inspection Request
Work inspection request
Very similar to IR, usually used for work activities such as blockwork, plaster, waterproofing, tiling, painting, ceilings or facade installation. It links the executed work to the approved shop drawing, method statement and ITP.
MIR

Submitted when materials arrive on site. The inspector checks material condition, quantity, labels, batch numbers, certificates, storage, expiry dates and whether the delivered item matches the approved material submittal.

Issued when work, material or process does not comply with approved drawings, specifications, method statements, ITP, codes or approved samples. It requires corrective action, root cause review and formal closure before the item is accepted.

Preventive action
Issued when a risk of non-conformance is identified before the defect actually occurs. It forces the team to improve controls, supervision, protection or method before the issue becomes a real failure.

Identifies defective work that must be rectified. It can be issued during construction, at snagging stage, or during the defects liability period depending on contract conditions.

Punch List

Commonly used in handover stages to identify all remaining actions before practical completion or taking-over. It may include finishing, MEP testing, documentation, cleaning and incomplete installations.

Section 12
Practical Construction Workflow

How the main documents connect from the start of an activity to handover. It is not a fixed rule for every project, but it represents the normal logic used in consultant and contractor construction control.

1 — Before Procurement Documents typically requiredMaterial Submittal, TDS, compliance statement, samples, supplier PQD Purpose of the stageConfirm that proposed materials and suppliers are compliant before money is spent and before wrong products arrive on site.
2 — Before Execution Documents typically requiredIFC/AFC drawings, shop drawings, method statement, ITP, risk assessment, permits Purpose of the stageMake sure the site team knows exactly what to build, how to build it, how to inspect it and how to do it safely.
3 — Material Delivery Documents typically requiredMIR, delivery note, COC, test certificates, storage inspection Purpose of the stageVerify that delivered material matches the approved submittal and is undamaged, traceable and properly stored.
4 — Work Execution Documents typically requiredChecklists, site supervision records, daily reports, progress photos Purpose of the stageControl workmanship while the activity is happening, not only after the mistake is already built.
5 — Formal Inspection Documents typically requiredIR/WIR, ITP references, approved drawings, checklists, test records Purpose of the stageObtain formal approval before work is covered, closed or continued to the next stage.
6 — Non-Compliance Control Documents typically requiredNCR, SOR, CAR, corrective action evidence, re-inspection IR Purpose of the stageRecord defects, stop repeated errors, correct the work and close the issue with evidence.
7 — Progress & Commercial Control Documents typically requiredDPR, WPR, MPR, updated program, payment application, VO/EOT/claim records Purpose of the stageLink actual site events to time, cost and contractual status. Good records protect both contractor and consultant.
8 — Handover Documents typically requiredAs-built drawings, O&M manuals, warranties, commissioning reports, training records, snag closure Purpose of the stageTransfer a complete, maintainable and legally documented asset to the client / facility team.
Typical Document Sequence
IFC / AFC Drawings→ Shop Drawings→ Material Submittal→ Method Statement→ ITP→ Mock-up→ MIR→ IR / WIR→ Approval / Comments / Rejection→ NCR / Snag Closure→ As-Built→ O&M→ Handover

Section 15
Final Summary — What Each Document Controls
Method Statement — controls execution
It tells the site team how to perform the work correctly and safely.
ITP — controls inspection & acceptance
It tells QA/QC and the consultant when to inspect, what to test, and what acceptance criteria apply.
Shop Drawing — controls buildability
It converts design intent into detailed site installation information.
Material Submittal — controls material compliance
It ensures that only approved materials, systems and suppliers are used.
IR / WIR — controls formal acceptance of work
It creates a signed inspection record before the activity is closed or continued.
MIR — controls delivered material
It prevents wrong, damaged, unapproved or expired materials from entering the works.
RFI / TQ — controls uncertainty
It prevents site teams from guessing when drawings or specifications are unclear.
NCR — controls non-compliance
It records defects and forces corrective action before acceptance.
SI / CVI — controls instructions
It documents instructions that may change scope, sequence, time or cost.
DPR / WPR / MPR — control progress records
They prove what happened on site and support management, payment, delay and claims.
As-Built & O&M — control handover
They transfer the final constructed asset and operating knowledge to the client.
Closing Statement

Construction documents are the control language of the site. They protect quality, safety, time, cost and final handover. A strong site architect, QA/QC engineer or package lead must know not only the name of each document, but also when to use it, what risk it controls and what evidence is required to close it.
