Introduction

The Room Where Everything Must Fit

What changes when a surgical robot moves into the operating theatre?

An operating theatre is already the most demanding room in a hospital: ultra-clean air, a sterile field that must never be crossed, a full team moving around a patient under anaesthesia, and dozens of devices drawing power, gases and data. Robotic-assisted surgery adds three large pieces of equipment — a surgeon console, a patient-side cart with robotic arms, and a vision / video tower — plus their cables, docking space and storage.

The architecture has to answer one question first: can the team see, reach and move around the patient with the robot docked, and still convert to open surgery in seconds? Room size, ceiling layout, pendant positions, door widths and the sterile workflow all follow from that answer.

Section 01

What Is Robotic-Assisted Surgery?

In robotic-assisted surgery the surgeon operates through small incisions using instruments held by robotic arms. The surgeon sits at a console in the same room, viewing a magnified 3D image from an endoscopic camera and controlling the instruments with hand controls and foot pedals. The robot does not act on its own — it translates the surgeon's movements, filters tremor and scales motion. [1]

At the bedside, a scrubbed assistant and scrub nurse exchange instruments and manage the ports; the anaesthetist stays at the head of the patient. Three working positions — console, bedside and anaesthesia — must keep eye and voice contact across a room crowded with equipment.

Section 02

Clinical Role & Specialties

Robotic surgery is used mainly for minimally invasive procedures in confined anatomy, where precision and visualisation matter most. In a cancer centre it is closely tied to oncology pathways: [1, 2]

Urology
Radical prostatectomy, partial nephrectomy, cystectomy — historically the largest robotic caseload.
Gynaecology
Hysterectomy, myomectomy, endometriosis and gynaecological oncology staging.
Colorectal & General Surgery
Rectal and colon resections, hernia repair, cholecystectomy, bariatric and upper-GI surgery.
Thoracic
Lobectomy and mediastinal tumour resection.
Head & Neck
Transoral robotic surgery (TORS) for selected throat tumours.
Oncology Pathway
Surgery is often one step in a pathway that includes imaging (MRI), PET-CT and radiotherapy — the theatre suite sits close to recovery, ICU and sterile services.

Section 04

Zoning & Sterile Flows

A theatre suite is organised as a cascade of cleanliness. Clean supplies move in one direction, used instruments and waste move out by a separate route, and air pressure steps down from the theatre to the corridor so air always flows from clean to less clean. [2, 3]

Unrestricted
Reception, waiting, changing entry — street clothes allowed
Semi-Restricted
Theatre corridor, anaesthesia room, recovery, stores — scrubs and caps
Restricted
Operating theatre, scrub area, sterile preparation — masks, highest pressure
Dirty / Disposal
Used instruments and waste — out via pass-through or disposal route to sterile services
  • Sterile storage feeds the theatre directly; used instruments leave by a separate disposal room or pass-through.
  • Scrub station at the theatre entrance with view into the room.
  • Patient enters via the anaesthesia room (where used) or directly through a wide automatic sliding door.
  • Theatre at positive pressure to the corridor; doors kept closed during surgery to protect the airflow.
  • Observation window or camera for teaching and coordination without entering the room.

Section 07

Room Layout, Ceiling & Finishes

Ceiling Coordination
The reflected ceiling plan holds the laminar-flow / supply diffuser over the table, two surgical lights, the anaesthesia and surgical pendants, monitors, LED panels, return grilles and fire detection. Pendant arms and lights must clear each other and the robot's arms at full swing.
Structure
Ceiling slab and steel support for lights and pendants, designed for their loads and deflection limits. The da Vinci-type patient cart is a mobile floor unit — the "ceiling-mounted arm" shown on Board 02 applies only if a ceiling-mounted robotic system is chosen.
Walls
Seamless antibacterial HPL or glass panels with minimal joints, flush-fitted cabinets, viewing window, X-ray viewers / displays and bumper rails.
Floors
Seamless antibacterial / antistatic sheet vinyl, coved up the wall; welded joints; level threshold for carts.
Doors
Wide hermetic automatic sliding door for beds and the patient cart; staff door to the scrub area; vision panels.
Colour & Light
Calm blue / white palette as in the visualisations; high colour-rendering task lighting, dimmable ambient light for endoscopic and console work.

Section 09

Safety Considerations

  • Surgical Safety Checklist — Sign In, Time Out and Sign Out built into the workflow and visible in the room. [4, 5]
  • Emergency undocking & conversion — space and routes for rapid undocking and conversion to open surgery; the robot must be removable from the bedside without obstruction.
  • Infection control — airflow protection of the sterile field, closed doors, cleanable finishes, separate clean and dirty routes. [3, 7]
  • Surgical fire — oxygen-enriched atmosphere plus energy devices and alcohol preps; team protocols and extinguishing equipment.
  • Electrical safety — isolated supply, equipotential bonding, cable routing that avoids trip hazards.
  • Communication — the console surgeon is not at the bedside; voice, video and line of sight across the room are part of safety.
  • Ergonomics — console height and display positions, standing space for the bedside team, monitor sight lines.

Conclusion

Designing for Precision and People

A robotic surgery suite succeeds when the building disappears behind the procedure: clean air arriving over the table, clean and dirty routes that never cross, ceiling equipment that never collides, and enough floor space for the robot, the team and an emergency at the same time.

The illustrated scheme sets out a clear zoning, a calm material palette and a coordinated ceiling. The next design step is to test it with the chosen robotic system's room templates — the equipment, not the drawing, sets the minimum room size.
